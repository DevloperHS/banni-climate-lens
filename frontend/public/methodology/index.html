What the satellite actually measures

Not colour — two kinds of light bouncing back. Leaves eat red light and throw back infrared. Bare soil throws back both about equally.

Left: living plants

Leaves absorb red light and reflect a lot of infrared. Little red plus lots of infrared means a high NDVI.

Example: Like a picky mirror: it keeps the red and sends the infrared back.

Right: bare salt soil

Soil reflects red and infrared about equally. The two arrows look alike, so NDVI stays near 0.

Example: Like a plain mirror: it sends both back the same.

A thicker arrow means more of that light bounced back.

NDVI = (infrared − red) ÷ (infrared + red)near 0 on bare ground, higher where more plants are alive

Move the slider: the ground goes from bare soil to a few tufts to a full sward, and the two arrows swap thickness. The bands on the scale are the rule of thumb used in dryland work; their exact boundaries differ between studies.

Pour water into one arm and the other rises. Rain and greenness work the same way: more rain, more green.

Take the rain away and see what is left

For every year we subtract the greenness that rain alone explains. If rain were the whole story, what remains would be flat noise around zero.

Green bars: greener than rain can explain. Red bars: less green. The dashed line is the overall direction.
