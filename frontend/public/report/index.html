1 · What we set out to detect

A single question, stated before any data were examined: after the effect of rainfall is removed, does the greenness of the Banni Grasslands still show a trend between 2001 and 2024, and can the cause of any such trend be identified from satellite data alone?

The first half of that question is answerable with satellite measurements. The second half, as this report shows, is not, and we report it as unresolved rather than filling it with a plausible story.

2 · Problem statement

Banni is a seasonally flooded, saline grassland on the southern edge of the Great Rann of Kachchh in Gujarat, India, and the grazing base of the pastoral Maldhari community. Satellite greenness over such drylands is dominated by rainfall, so a rise in greenness is not by itself evidence that the land is improving, and a fall is not by itself evidence of degradation.

The established way to separate the two is residual trend analysis, or RESTREND: regress greenness on rainfall, then test whether the residuals — the part rainfall does not account for — trend over time.[1] The method was introduced for exactly this problem in the Syrian drylands and has since been applied widely in semi-arid rangelands.[2] Its documented limitation is the one that governs this report: it can flag that something other than rainfall is acting, but it cannot say what.[2]

3 · Prior work

Three strands of published work bear directly on this question.

Land cover. The introduced shrub Prosopis juliflora was planted in Banni from the 1960s and has since expanded over a large share of the grassland; reported figures are about 6 per cent of the area in 1997 rising to about 54 per cent by 2015, alongside a reported spread of salinity.[4] A 2024 land-suitability assessment of Banni states plainly that it remains unclear whether Prosopis invasion or rising salinity is the primary cause of degradation, and warns that NDVI-only assessments made without soil information can misstate degradation.[5]

Vegetation response to climate. A 2025 study combined Landsat NDVI with the Standardized Precipitation Index over Banni for 1987–2022 and found a significant positive NDVI–SPI association for C4 grasses, while C3 plants including Neltuma (Prosopis) juliflora showed greater resistance during dry events; the authors conclude that the invasion may temporarily stabilise greenness during dry periods while risking conversion to shrubland.[6] That study did not report a long-term rainfall-adjusted trend.

Method. RESTREND and the Mann–Kendall family of trend tests are standard for this class of problem.[1][2][7][8]

4.2 Biological basis of the measurement

NDVI is not a photograph of colour. Chlorophyll in a functioning leaf absorbs strongly in the red and the leaf’s internal mesophyll structure scatters strongly in the near infrared, so living canopy produces a large gap between the two bands, while bare or saline soil returns the two bands at similar levels. NDVI is the normalised form of that gap, (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red).

Two consequences matter for the interpretation of every result below. First, the index responds to the amount of functioning green canopy, not to its botanical identity: a dense stand of Prosopis and a healthy sward of grass can return similar values. Second, C3 woody species with deeper roots hold canopy through dry spells that cause C4 grasses to senesce, so a rising trend in greenness is compatible with an increase in woody cover and with an improvement in grass, and the index cannot separate the two.[6]

4.3 Statistical procedure

Write Nt for the monsoon-season mean NDVI of year t and Rt for the monsoon rainfall total of the same year. The procedure is:

(i) Fit. Ordinary least squares of greenness on rainfall, Nt = a + b Rt + et, giving the share of greenness that rainfall accounts for, reported as R2.

(ii) Residual. et = Nt − (a + b Rt). Under the null hypothesis that rainfall is the only driver, et is noise with no trend.

(iii) Slope. Theil–Sen estimator on the residuals: the median of all pairwise slopes (ej − ei) / (j − i) for i < j.[8] A median of slopes is used rather than a least-squares slope because it is not dragged by one unusual year.

(iv) Significance. Mann–Kendall test on the residual series. Because yearly vegetation series are often serially correlated, which inflates the apparent significance of a trend, the platform reports the Hamed–Rao variance-corrected form of the test alongside the plain one.[7]

(v) Uncertainty. Confidence intervals in Figure 5 are percentile intervals from resampling the year pairs, 2000 replicates, computed in the browser from the same series the figures plot.

Every figure on this page is drawn from data fetched live from the platform API at page load. The statistics are recomputed in the browser from those series, so a reader can compare them against the platform’s own server-side numbers; the two are independent implementations of the same definitions.

4.4 Implementation

Series extraction runs on Google Earth Engine; aggregation, trend tests and the rainfall adjustment run in Python with NumPy, pandas, SciPy and pymannkendall on the server; the figures on this page recompute the same quantities in plain JavaScript with no external library. Outputs are served as JSON over the platform API and are downloadable as CSV, GeoJSON and GeoTIFF.

7 · What is new in this work

We claim three contributions, each narrow.

A rainfall-adjusted trend for Banni over the MODIS era. The studies we located report the NDVI–rainfall relationship, or land-cover change, but not a residual trend with its significance for 2001–2024. This report supplies that number with its test statistic and its uncertainty.

Systematic elimination rather than attribution. Most of the effort went into removing explanations rather than proposing one: a second sensor, a second vegetation index, several rainfall formulations, the timing of the rain within the season, and the location of the analysis window were each tested and each left the residual in place. The elimination list is reported in full, including the four candidates that could not be tested at all.

A direct, if weak, test of the Prosopis hypothesis. The dominant explanation in the regional literature is invasion. We compared greenness trends between areas classified as high and low Prosopis and found no significant difference. The classification covers one year only, so this neither confirms nor refutes the hypothesis; we report it because the test is rarely made explicit.

We do not claim a new method, a new dataset, or the identification of a cause.
